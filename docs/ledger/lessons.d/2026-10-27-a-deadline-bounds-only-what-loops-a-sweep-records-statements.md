## 2026-10-27 — a progress-handler deadline bounds only what loops, its opcode counter belongs to one prepared statement (re-running one text carries it, distinct texts never add up), and a sweep for the class records the statements instead of reading the call sites

**What was found.** #1543 found that a bare `count(*)` inside a `statement_deadline` block is one VM opcode the progress handler is never asked inside. The follow-on was to ask where else that was true, in 58 call sites across `src/`. Reading the sites would have meant following ORM `.count()` calls, a count over a view (`keyword_mentions_all` flattens to the table), scalar subqueries and a dozen helper layers by eye. Recording found it in one test run: a pytest plugin wrapped `statement_deadline`, noted the call site, and for every statement executed while a block was open ran `EXPLAIN` and kept the opcode list: 2,557 distinct (site, statement) shapes from 23 sites, of which eight compiled to `OP_Count`. Two were in the Library figures path and the encrypted store's normal storage-composition path; the first documented its 300 s deadline as protecting a pooled connection and a WAL-pinning read snapshot, and under the real deadline at a millionth of a second it ran whole over 1,240,657 rows. The sites the suite never reached (35 of 58) were classified by reading and are marked as the weaker basis.

**The rules.**

1. **Sweep for an uninterruptible-statement class by recording the opcodes of what actually runs, then prove each suspect with the real deadline at a tiny value over enough rows.** The recording found a count that reading would have taken for a loop (a view, an ORM `.count()`), and it also over-reports (an unfiltered and a filtered `query(Model).count()` look alike by name and differ in opcode): a recorded flag is a suspect, never a finding, until the real-deadline run says "ran whole" and the handler's consultation count (0 against hundreds for the loop spelling) agrees. State what the recording covered (here 23 of 58 sites, shapes the tests happen to use).
2. **The handler's 20,000-opcode counter belongs to a PREPARED STATEMENT, not to the connection.** Re-running one SQL text carries it across runs, because the driver hands back the cached prepared statement; distinct texts never add up. Handler at 20,000, raw `sqlite3` (3.45.1) and `sqlcipher3` (SQLite 3.51.1) alike: ten DISTINCT `count(1)` statements of 15,010 opcodes each (150,000 in all) consulted it 0 times, ten runs of one text 7 times, and 4,000 distinct short statements 0 times. Through the repo's real `statement_deadline(…, 1e-6)`: 400 runs of one probe text (about 18 opcodes each, a 5,000-row scratch table, plain SQLite 3.45.1) ran whole in 49 ms and 4,000 raised, while 4,000 and 20,000 DISTINCT short statements (1.8 s for the latter) ran whole. So a "bounded by the statement deadline" docstring over a loop of short probes is false; a block of distinct short statements (an f-string table loop, the `_COUNTED_TABLES` loop, ORM lazy loads) is not bounded by the deadline whatever their number; and the honest bound is the loop's own count, which the docstring should say. The first version of this rule said the opcodes "accumulate across the statements of one connection"; the read of this change measured it and they do not. A test that respells the one long statement bare can also show it: the whole 400-probe estimate came back complete under the 1e-6 s deadline.
3. **A probe must isolate the unit it claims to time, and says "not isolated" when it cannot.** The first sorter probe returned 300,000 rows to Python, so its "sort phase" was a difference between two noisy Python fetches (a 194 ms figure that was within the 1.7 to 2.3 s run-to-run spread of the scan itself); it was withdrawn rather than reported. An aggregating outer query (`SELECT max(x) FROM (... ORDER BY x LIMIT n)`) removes the fetch, and where even that cannot separate the unit from the scan, no figure is claimed.
4. **Count the price of the loop form where the bare form is a speed-up you are about to remove, and do not extrapolate it.** `count(id)` over 1.24 M mentions cost 135 ms cold against 93 ms and 45 ms warm against 1 ms (about 36 ns per row of CPU). That is the whole price and it is small next to a count that needs 55 to 182 s of disk at 12 M rows, but those field figures were taken on the bare statement: mark them superseded, pending, and do not carry the 36 ns across a size nobody measured.
5. **A recording keyed on (deadline block, statement text) cannot tell two callers of identical SQL apart, and a call site's wrapper is a fact about its CALLER.** The sweep's recording DID see `SELECT COUNT(*) FROM keyword_mentions` under the bundle's per-member block (`bundle.py:2921`, n = 2, from the suite's walk of the bundle): `performance.py:100` and `benchmark.py:417` emit that same text, the two merged into one shape, the shape was attributed to the first, and the second was classified by reading its function, which has no deadline of its own, as "no deadline around it". RC2 found it while building the test for the neighbouring count. Before writing "no deadline around it" for a source site, find its callers; and where a recorded shape's text is emitted by more than one site, the recording is evidence for all of them. A second pass (all 57 bundle members through `_write_all_diagnostics_zip`, run after the fix) shows `benchmark.json` with `count(1)` and found six more bare counts in `performance.json`, attributed the same way, by statement identity, because the recorder keeps the stack of deadline blocks, not of calls. State what such a pass could not see: a 15 MiB store, four members that ran no statement, and members that open their own connection (invisible, because it records only while a block is open). A wrapper that hides the closure the harness inspects (`_member_touches_db` tests `"db" in fn.__code__.co_freevars`) changes which path the member takes: the first run of the pass saw 8 of 57 members for that reason alone.
