## 2026-10-27 — a progress-handler deadline bounds only what loops, its opcode count accumulates across statements, and a sweep for the class records the statements instead of reading the call sites

**What was found.** #1543 found that a bare `count(*)` inside a `statement_deadline` block is one VM opcode the progress handler is never asked inside. The follow-on was to ask where else that was true, in 58 call sites across `src/`. Reading the sites would have meant following ORM `.count()` calls, a count over a view (`keyword_mentions_all` flattens to the table), scalar subqueries and a dozen helper layers by eye. Recording found it in one test run: a pytest plugin wrapped `statement_deadline`, noted the call site, and for every statement executed while a block was open ran `EXPLAIN` and kept the opcode list: 2,557 distinct (site, statement) shapes from 23 sites, of which eight compiled to `OP_Count`. Two were in the Library figures path and the encrypted store's normal storage-composition path; the first documented its 300 s deadline as protecting a pooled connection and a WAL-pinning read snapshot, and under the real deadline at a millionth of a second it ran whole over 1,240,657 rows. The sites the suite never reached (35 of 58) were classified by reading and are marked as the weaker basis.

**The rules.**

1. **Sweep for an uninterruptible-statement class by recording the opcodes of what actually runs, then prove each suspect with the real deadline at a tiny value over enough rows.** The recording found a count that reading would have taken for a loop (a view, an ORM `.count()`), and it also over-reports (an unfiltered and a filtered `query(Model).count()` look alike by name and differ in opcode): a recorded flag is a suspect, never a finding, until the real-deadline run says "ran whole" and the handler's consultation count (0 against hundreds for the loop spelling) agrees. State what the recording covered (here 23 of 58 sites, shapes the tests happen to use).
2. **The handler's 20,000 opcodes accumulate across the statements of one connection.** A block of many short statements is asked only once their total passes the interval: 400 statements of about 30 opcodes ran whole under a 1e-6 s deadline (49 ms) and 4,000 raised, plain SQLite 3.45.1. A "bounded by the statement deadline" docstring over a loop of short probes is false, and the honest bound is the loop's own count; say so in the docstring. A test that respells the one long statement bare can also show it: the whole 400-probe estimate came back complete under the 1e-6 s deadline.
3. **A probe must isolate the unit it claims to time, and says "not isolated" when it cannot.** The first sorter probe returned 300,000 rows to Python, so its "sort phase" was a difference between two noisy Python fetches (a 194 ms figure that was within the 1.7 to 2.3 s run-to-run spread of the scan itself); it was withdrawn rather than reported. An aggregating outer query (`SELECT max(x) FROM (... ORDER BY x LIMIT n)`) removes the fetch, and where even that cannot separate the unit from the scan, no figure is claimed.
4. **Count the price of the loop form where the bare form is a speed-up you are about to remove, and do not extrapolate it.** `count(id)` over 1.24 M mentions cost 135 ms cold against 93 ms and 45 ms warm against 1 ms (about 36 ns per row of CPU). That is the whole price and it is small next to a count that needs 55 to 182 s of disk at 12 M rows, but those field figures were taken on the bare statement: mark them superseded, pending, and do not carry the 36 ns across a size nobody measured.
