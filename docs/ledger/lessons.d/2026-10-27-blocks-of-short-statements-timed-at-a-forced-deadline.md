## 2026-10-27 — a deadline's gap is measured by forcing the block to a millionth of a second and timing how long it stays open; no block the walks reached is held open by many distinct short statements, and the stretches it cannot stop are listed with their sizes and the connections and routes the recording could not see

**What was found.** #1543 and #1563 proved a class: the progress handler's 20,000-opcode counter belongs to one prepared statement, so a block of many DISTINCT short statements runs whole under any deadline (20,000 of them, 1.8 s under a deadline of a millionth of a second). The question left was whether any block in `src/` is such a block at a size that matters. Counting statements by reading was not going to answer it; forcing every `statement_deadline` to 1e-6 s (the wrapper installed before the app imports, so every module binds it; a block that asks for 0 is left alone, since 0 means disabled) and timing how long each block stayed open did: if the handler is consulted at all it raises at the first consultation, so the open time IS the stretch the deadline cannot stop. Each block also recorded the SQL time, the longest single call, the statement count, the DISTINCT text count, whether it exited by exception, and any connection opened inside it (the counts come from SQLAlchemy Engine events; a statement on a raw connection is timed but not counted). **The rules.**

1. **To find what a deadline cannot stop, force it to a millionth of a second and time how long the block stays open, split into SQL, longest single call, Python and distinct texts, instead of reading or counting statements.** Many short statements shows as open about equal to the block with small SQL, a small longest call and a large distinct count; one long statement as longest call about equal to SQL about equal to open; Python as open much greater than SQL. Wrap the cursors of EVERY driver the app uses (`sqlite3` and `sqlcipher3`): the first encrypted run had wall times only until the second driver was wrapped. Say what the walks did not reach (the last two tables above) beside the answer, because "no block" means "no block reached".
2. **The class is bounded when the statement count is set by code and schema, and that is a property to re-check, not to assume.** The distinct-text count was identical between the 60,000- and the 300,000-article store in 77 of 81 cases (and `source-audit.json` is the exception that went DOWN, 26 to 9); at 9.9 M mentions no block had more than the same 285. A search of `src/` for SQL that inlines a data value into its text (`literal_binds`, an f-string carrying a value) found none in a deadline block; where texts differ only by an `IN (?, ?, ...)` length the chunk loops give two sizes. At about 90 microseconds a statement (plain, warm, the #1563 probe) reaching the 60 s shared deadline takes about 660,000 distinct statements in ONE block and the 300 s member deadline about 3.3 million; cold and encrypted the schema-drift block ran its 285 texts at about 1.6 ms each (0.6 ms of it SQL), which would reach 60 s at about 38,000. The conclusion holds at either rate, against 285 for the largest. A change that builds one statement per DATA value in a deadline block re-opens the class.
3. **A progress callback names the item that is STARTING, so what the harness collected at call k belongs to item k-1.** The first version of the bundle harness attributed every block one member late. The invariant that catches it, and holds on every row of the saved runs, is that no block's open time may exceed its member's wall; the SUM of a member's block times may exceed it, because blocks nest (the member block wraps `storage.py:304`). Separately, the first draft of the sweep quoted shipped member times (13.74, 10.91 and 8.10 s) that match no saved row; the saved rows give 14.11, 8.98 and 8.31 s, and the draft's figures were withdrawn. Take figures from saved rows, never from a log or from memory.
4. **Do not close a measured gap of 0.25 s with a mechanism in the file the whole app runs through.** A `before_cursor_execute` expiry check installed by `statement_deadline` (every new statement after expiry raises) would close the class by construction for one `monotonic()` call per statement, but it changes `maintenance.py` for every caller and would remove a stretch of 0.25 s. It is a proposal if a block ever appears with more than a few thousand distinct statements, and not before.
5. **This probe's box cannot show a disk-bound statement, and says so.** Cold against warm differed by under 0.2 s for a 186 MiB index read off a virtual disk; the cold numbers above are the cost of the first read plus SQLCipher's key derivation and decryption, not of a spinning or contended disk. They were taken on one fresh-laid-out synthetic store (1,000 distinct `observed_on` dates), about 1.3 to 1.6 times slower than the field boxes on CPU work; nothing here is extrapolated to a size, a disk or a load nobody measured. The raw rows are in the WAL lane's notes (`distinct-statement-sweep-data/`, `mentions-count-data/`), outside the repository.
