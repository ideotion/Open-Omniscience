## 2026-10-17 — A DURATION STATISTIC RECORDED WHEN THE EVENT ENDS CANNOT SEE THE EVENT THAT NEVER ENDS: KEEP CUMULATIVE TOTALS, READ BY DIFFERENCE, READ THE CLOCK UNDER THE LOCK THAT ORDERS THEM, AND CHECK THE GAUGE AGAINST AN INDEPENDENT LOG

**What was found.** `lock_stats` recorded a hold in its pass window when the hold ENDED, and the pass monitor resets the window at the start of every pass. So the one case the extraction lock's diagnostics exist for, a hold that does not end (a hang, an 85 s page, a pass that crashes), leaves nothing in the window; every other extraction waits behind it and the record shows a quiet lock. A mean and a maximum taken at the end of events are blind to exactly the event still in progress.

**The shape that works (Item C, `src/ingest/lock_stats.py`).**
1. **Keep CUMULATIVE totals that only grow (`occupied_s`, `waited_s`) and give the reader a reading that adds the part still open (`live_reading()`).** The difference of two readings is then exact for the interval between them, whatever happened inside it, resets of the pass window included (`reset()` deliberately leaves the gauge alone, test-pinned). Ship the totals and let the reader divide; a rate stored in the gauge would be right for one interval only, the one it was computed over.
2. **Keep the open waiters in a DICT keyed by thread, not as a running sum of start times.** `(a + b) - b != a` in floating point, so a sum drifts; a dict is subtracted from `now` at reading time and drifts by nothing.
3. **Read the clock UNDER the gauge's own lock, in the mark and in the reading.** If a mark read the clock outside the lock, a reading could get a time earlier than a mark already applied and report more held time than elapsed. One private leaf lock (never held while taking another) totally orders marks and readings.
4. **A mark that sits inside the hold lengthens the hold by its own cost: measure it, and measure it at two levels.** The three marks of one extraction cost 1.5 microseconds together (about 1 microsecond inside the hold) on the real functions; a 14-collector run of the real collector, base tree against gauge tree, alternating, three runs per cell, showed no difference in throughput, CPU per article or mean hold beyond the spread of the three (the figures are in the shipped row). The micro number says what the marks cost, the pass number says whether anything notices; neither stands in for the other.
5. **Test the gauge against an independent log, not against itself.** `tests/test_lock_live_reading.py` hammers the real `extract_article` path from eight threads with a fake clock and builds the expected totals from a tape of the gauge's own clock reads (six per extraction, keyed by thread NAME: thread idents are reused, and the first version of the check also counted the test thread's own final `live_reading()` as an extraction and failed its read-count assertion for a reason that had nothing to do with the gauge). 18 mutants (clock outside the lock, open hold dropped, wait not closed at the hold, release not in a `finally`, marks swapped, ...) are all killed. The harness cross-check on real runs agrees too: the gauge's growth over the measured passes equals the pass window's hold and wait totals to within 1.5 ms over totals of 22 to 615 s.
6. **Say what the gauge cannot promise, in the code.** An unmatched release is clamped; a `KeyboardInterrupt` landing exactly inside `released()` can leave a phantom holder. Best effort, and a failed mark never replaces an extraction's result (one `except Exception:` in one helper).

**What the A/B did not show, and why that is written down.** The longest single hold and the longest single wait read higher in two of three gauge runs at 8 KB pages (median 0.75 against 0.56 s, and 0.95 against 0.77 s). It is one event per run, absent at 64 KB where the same marks run, and the mean hold did not move, so it is read as scheduler noise on four shared vCPUs; with three runs a small effect on a single event is not excluded.
