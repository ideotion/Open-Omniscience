## 2026-10-26 — A "keep the richest N" bound applied after `.all()` bounds the answer and not the memory: stream the read, keep a heap, and measure the heap with tracemalloc

**What was found (audit 0.5.0, A-003).** `stories.article_keyword_sets` read every mention row of the period with one `.all()`, grouped them per article, and only then did `build_stories` keep the 4,000 richest articles. The 4,000 was a bound on the clustering and on the payload, not on what the read held: on written corpora the resident rise of the read grew with the corpus (712 MB at 1.56 M mentions, 1,132 MB at 3.12 M, 1,971 MB at 6.24 M, 3,650 MB at 12.48 M, plain store) and the Python heap alone was 417.8 MB at 1.56 M mentions, about 268 B a mention row. The live store's yearly period is about 20.9 M rows. The read ran inside the request that built the edition.

**The shape of the fix.** The period's article ids come from `ix_article_observed` (one sorted `array('q')`, 8 B an article); their mentions are read 900 articles at a time through the covering index `ix_mention_article_count (article_id, count, keyword_id)`; each article is cut to its top 12 visible keywords as the chunk arrives (stoplist verdicts cached per keyword, the cache emptied at 100,000 entries); a heap keyed `(len(terms), -aid)` keeps the richest 4,000. Memory follows the chunk and the heap, not the corpus: 0.26 to 0.31 GB plain and 0.10 to 0.13 GB encrypted at 1.56 M and at 12.48 M mentions, and the read is not slower (cold 1.3x faster at 20 k articles, 1.9x at 160 k). Numbers and method: `audit-0.5.0/inputs/remeasure-stories-export/REPORT.md`.

**Rules.**
1. **When the downstream step is "keep the top N", the read is a stream and the top N is a heap.** A bound applied to a list that was already materialised is a bound on the output. Ask of every `.all()` followed by a slice: what is held at the moment of the slice?
2. **Measure Python memory with `tracemalloc`; RSS is for the process.** The plain store's RSS includes the mmap'd database pages a query touches, so the plain floor sits above the encrypted one for the same code. A test of the memory claim runs the read under `tracemalloc` and bounds the peak per chunk (it fails on a copy of the old read).
3. **A chunk size and a deadline are numbers that name what they protect, measured on written state.** 900 protects SQLite's 999-variable limit and one read's heap (30.2 / 33.6 / 45.7 MB at chunks 100 / 300 / 900) and is also the fastest of the three; the deadline protects the connection the edition holds, was measured at 3.0 us a row cold (2.6 warm) and extrapolated, labelled, to 64 s at the field's 20.9 M rows; 600 s is about nine times that. Extrapolations carry their word.
4. **Equality of the answer is checked on the answer.** The generator's text yields no stories, so a story comparison compared nothing: the benchmark digests the kept sets, and the unit tests run a hand-made world through a copy of the old read as an oracle. A world without boundary articles (an article at 23:59:59 and one at 00:00:00 of the next day, a verdict cache that must be emptied mid-read) let two mutants survive; the world now carries them.
5. **Say the one thing that changed.** Mention rows without an article row ("orphans") used to be counted in `articles_in_period` and no longer are, because the ids now come from the article table; the payload also gained `read_bound`, naming the cap, the terms per article, the articles per read and the deadline.
