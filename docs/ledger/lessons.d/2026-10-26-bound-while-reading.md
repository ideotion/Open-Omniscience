## 2026-10-26 — A "keep the richest N" bound applied after `.all()` bounds the answer and not the memory: stream the read, keep a heap, measure the heap with tracemalloc, and say what the new read selects BY

**What was found (audit 0.5.0, A-003).** `stories.article_keyword_sets` read every mention row of the period with one `.all()`, grouped them per article, and only then did `build_stories` keep the 4,000 richest articles. The 4,000 was a bound on the clustering and on the payload, not on what the read held: on written corpora the resident rise of the read grew with the corpus (712 MB at 1.56 M mentions, 1,132 MB at 3.12 M, 1,971 MB at 6.24 M, 3,650 MB at 12.48 M, plain store) and the Python heap alone was 417.8 MB at 1.56 M mentions, about 268 B a mention row. The live store's yearly period is about 20.9 M rows. The read ran inside the request that built the edition.

**The shape of the fix.** The period's article ids come from `ix_article_observed` (one sorted `array('q')`, 8 B an article); their mentions are read 900 articles at a time through the covering index `ix_mention_article_count (article_id, count, keyword_id)`; each article is cut to its top 12 visible keywords as the chunk arrives (stoplist verdicts cached per keyword, the cache emptied at 100,000 entries); a heap keyed `(len(terms), -aid)` keeps the richest 4,000. Memory follows the chunk and the heap plus eight bytes for each article of the period: 0.26 to 0.31 GB plain and 0.10 to 0.13 GB encrypted at 1.56 M and at 12.48 M mentions, and the read is not slower (cold 1.3x faster at 20 k articles, 1.9x at 160 k). Numbers and method: `audit-0.5.0/inputs/remeasure-stories-export/REPORT.md`.

**Rules.**
1. **When the downstream step is "keep the top N", the read is a stream and the top N is a heap.** A bound applied to a list that was already materialised is a bound on the output. Ask of every `.all()` followed by a slice: what is held at the moment of the slice?
2. **Measure Python memory with `tracemalloc`; RSS is for the process.** The plain store's RSS includes the mmap'd database pages a query touches, so the plain floor sits above the encrypted one for the same code. A test of the memory claim runs the read under `tracemalloc` and compares GROWTH between two sizes (fixed costs both reads share cancel; a first small run warms them so they are not charged to the first measurement); it fails on a copy of the old read.
3. **A chunk size and a deadline are numbers that name what they protect, measured on written state.** 900 protects SQLite's 999-variable limit and one read's heap (30.2 / 33.6 / 45.7 MB at chunks 100 / 300 / 900) and is chosen as the bound, not as the fastest: the three are within noise on time (RC2's re-measure at 160 k cold, 40.7 / 38.1 / 40.9 s, a spread of about 7 % whose order flips between runs, where this lane's own run had 900 first); the deadline protects the connection the edition holds, was measured at 3.0 us a row cold (2.6 warm; RC2: 3.27 to 3.5) and extrapolated, labelled, to 64 s (RC2: 68 to 73 s) at the field's 20.9 M rows; 600 s is about eight times that. A claim that one setting is the fastest needs more than two runs whose order can flip. Extrapolations carry their word. A deadline test must make the read really run out of time (a world large enough to reach the progress handler's 20,000-opcode tick and a deadline of 1e-9 s), because a test that makes the read raise by itself passes with no deadline at all.
4. **Equality of the answer is checked on the answer, and on the right axis.** The generator's text yields no stories, so a story comparison compared nothing: the benchmark digests the kept sets, and the unit tests run a hand-made world through a copy of the old read as an oracle. A world without boundary articles (the period's first instant, a second before it ends, the instant it ends; a verdict cache that must be emptied mid-read) let two mutants survive; the world now carries them.
5. **Say what the new read selects BY, and prove the stated differences with a test.** The old read chose MENTION rows by their denormalised `observed_on`; the new one chooses ARTICLES by `coalesce(published_at, created_at)` (how `period.py` defines the period). They agree while an article's clock has not moved since it was indexed. They differ for an article whose clock moved after indexing (the law corpus's heal clears `published_at` without re-indexing), one whose mentions carry no date, and mention rows without an article row. The first version of this change claimed "identical except orphans" on the strength of a world where the two always agreed, and an independent read found the rest in the code; the world that proves a claim of equality has to contain the cases that would break it. A test now pins each difference (`test_the_period_follows_the_articles_clock_where_the_old_read_followed_each_mentions_date`), and the law heal that leaves stale mention dates is a 0.5.1 item (re-index there).
